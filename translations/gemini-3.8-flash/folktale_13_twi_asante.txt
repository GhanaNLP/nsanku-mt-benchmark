If it is cooked well, the rooster's beak is also delicious.

FATHER ANANSE lived there with his children: Afudotwedotwe (Belly-like-a-swollen-bag), Nyiwan-konfwea (Legs-like-slender-reeds), and Tikonokono (Head-like-a-huge-lump). Ananse told them, "Go look for some figs and bring them for me to eat." So these children set off; they walked, walked, walked, until they came upon some ripe figs, and the children went underneath the tree. Afudotwedotwe said to Nyiwan-konfwea, "Climb up and pluck some." He replied, "Ah! If I climb, my legs will break." Tikonokono said, "Afudotwedotwe, you climb instead." He said, "Ah! If I climb, my belly will burst, so you yourself climb." And Tikonokono climbed. He plucked and plucked the figs, then asked, "Is that enough?" The others replied, "Yes." But he plucked and plucked again; and he asked them once more, "Is that enough?" The others said, "Lower your head and look down." As he lowered his head to look down, his huge head pulled him to the ground, crash! Afudotwedotwe started to laugh, and his belly burst, pop! Nyiwankonfwea started to run off to tell his father, and his legs broke right behind the mound. A rooster, having bathed its soul, came out to discard its eggshells, only to see Nyiwankonfwea fallen there with his legs broken behind the mound. As it laughed, its beak broke:

He says he is laughing, with his mouth open wide.

He says he laughs, with his sharp mouth.”

That is why the elders say, "When someone falls, go look after them, and do not laugh." But as for the Rooster, he did just that and went to laugh, which is why his beak became pointed, whereas in the past his mouth was very beautiful.

This story of mine that I have told, whether it is sweet or not sweet, take some away, and bring some back.
