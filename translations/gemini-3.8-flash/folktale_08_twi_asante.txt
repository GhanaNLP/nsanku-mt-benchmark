A certain woman was pregnant, and she went to a certain deity to inquire. And the deity said to her, "Originally, if you give birth to this child, she will die, but I will make an effort for you, and what I tell you is this: the child is a queen mother's child in the eyes of God, and when you give birth to her, she must not do any work, and when you wake up, bathe her, rub her with oil, and take her and seat her on a stool." Indeed, she gave birth to her. Like a child of a folktale, it does not take long for them to grow up. The child was called Akosua Denta. Just as the deity had told the mother, when she woke up, she would bathe her, rub her with oil, cook something for her to eat, and then go to the farm. One day, however, she did not have the chance to cook anything for her to eat, so she said to her maidservant Kra, "I am going to the farm, so cook something for Akosua Denta to eat." Then the mother set off and went. When Kra finished cooking the plantain, she said, "Akosua Denta, come and pound this fufuo with your queenly ways." She said, "I will not come." Kra said, "Just come, with your peace of mind." Then Akosua Denta got up and went; she took the pestle and pounded the fufuo. As soon as she finished, she went to lie down in the room, and covered her head with a cloth. Kra went there and said, "Denta"; dead silence! She uncovered the cloth from her head; it was Akosua Denta who had died; she laid the cloth back over her. Before long, the mother returned from the farm. When she arrived, she did not look for her child, but went to the hearth, cooked food, and finished. Then she said, "Kra, where is Denta?" She said, "Nana, even the food I cooked and set before her is still sitting there." The mother said, "I hear." Then the mother set off and went into the room to take a cloth to go outside to search for her child. When she entered the room, something was lying there in a heap. She lifted the cloth and looked, and it was Akosua Denta who was dead. She said, "Kra, didn't you say Denta went outside, yet she is dead?" Kra said, "Nana, I didn't see." Then the woman wailed, strapped her child to her back, carried her out into the street, and wept; and the village chief came and said, "Bring your child so that we may go and bury her."
